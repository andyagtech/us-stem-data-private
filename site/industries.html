Five industries the country cannot offshore

An ore body is where it is. So is a well, an airframe line, a cell plant and a reactor. The work has to be done here, by people who are here. This page asks who the United States is training to do it.

Who runs it, once it is built

These five fields are measured by who gets the doctorate. The grid itself is not: electrician and lineworker credentials go almost entirely to Americans, so the question there is not who, but how many. The country awards fewer than a hundred power plant technology credentials a year, nationally.

What restricts this work, precisely

Some of this work carries federal access restrictions. Three different regimes get run together under the word “clearance”, and they are not the same thing. Export control turns on U.S. person status (22 CFR 120.62), which includes lawful permanent residents — it is not a clearance and does not require citizenship. Unescorted access at a commercial reactor (10 CFR 73.56) is a background and fitness-for-duty determination, also not a clearance. A security clearance is eligibility for access to classified information under EO 12968 and SEAD 4; DOE’s L and Q authorisations (10 CFR 710) are of that kind and do require citizenship.

Mining and metalsNone by defaultNo export-control or access-authorisation regime applies by default. Some critical-minerals work carries federal funding conditions, which are grant terms and not a personnel restriction.
Battery technologyNone by defaultNo export-control or access-authorisation regime applies by default, though specific DOE-funded programmes may attach their own conditions.
AerospaceExport controlMuch of this work is export-controlled. ITAR (22 CFR 120-130) limits access to defence articles and technical data to a 'U.S. person' as defined at 22 CFR 120.62: a citizen, a lawful permanent resident, or a protected individual. This is not a security clearance and it does not require citizenship — a permanent resident qualifies. It does exclude someone on a temporary visa, which is exactly who IPEDS counts as a non-resident alien.
NuclearAccess authorisationTwo different regimes, often confused. Unescorted access to a commercial reactor requires NRC access authorisation under 10 CFR 73.56 — a background investigation and fitness-for-duty determination, not a security clearance, and not restricted by citizenship on its face. DOE work on weapons-adjacent programmes requires an L or Q access authorisation under 10 CFR 710, which IS a clearance and does require US citizenship. Most civil nuclear engineering falls under the first, not the second.

One consequence is worth stating because it is easy to get backwards. IPEDS counts a permanent resident as domestic, so every non-resident alien in these figures is on a temporary visa and is therefore outside the U.S.-person definition that export control uses. That is a statement about what the rules say, not about who was hired: this data records a degree awarded and nothing more.

Method and limits

A CIP code is a field of study. It is not an industry, and nobody in this data is observed working anywhere. The mapping from field to industry is this site’s, and every page shows the codes it used so the mapping can be argued with.

  • A CIP code is a field of study. Nobody in this data is observed working in the industry it is matched to.
  • The battery entry is a proxy built from materials programmes, because IPEDS publishes no battery category.
  • Shares are of degrees awarded, not of the current workforce. A doctorate awarded in 2016 may have left the country or the field.
  • 'Non-resident alien' is the IPEDS reporting category: a student on a temporary visa. Permanent residents are counted as domestic.
  • The restriction note states the law as written. It is not an observed hiring outcome: this data cannot show who was employed, under what authorisation, or whether they stayed.
  • Export control, access authorisation and a security clearance are three different things. Only a DOE L or Q access authorisation requires US citizenship.
